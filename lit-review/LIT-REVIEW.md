# Literature review: prior work informing the Hinglish vs English GEO study

Collected 2026-08-14 via parallel research agents; every citation was located at its listed URL during collection. Structured data in lit-review.json.


## Lens: geo-foundations

The GEO literature began as a content-optimization problem and has matured into a measurement science, and the measurement half is what the EN-vs-Hinglish study should lean on. Aggarwal et al. (KDD 2024) established the canonical visibility metrics: word-count impression share, position-adjusted word count with exponential position decay (Imp_pwc = sum over citing sentences of |s|*e^(-pos(s)/|S|), normalized by response length), and an LLM-judged subjective impression across seven dimensions. Everything since either refines the outcome variable (C-SEO Bench and the Berkeley ranking-manipulation paper move it to rank-in-answer for product recommendations; the 2026 USC GEO-Bench formalizes rank movement as Normalized Rank Gain and Success@alpha) or interrogates its reliability. All these metrics are computable from captured answer text plus citation lists, so the study can adopt them wholesale: brand-level mention share, position-weighted share, rank within the recommendation list, and Jaccard/RBO overlap of cited domains.

The single most important methodological development for this study is the 2026 variance literature. St. Gallen's "Don't Measure Once" showed that identical prompts rerun simultaneously overlap only 32-43% on cited sources (brands are steadier at 45-59%), that day-over-day source turnover is roughly 65%, and that per-brand detection rates stabilize only around 7-8 runs per prompt; Sielinski's bootstrap framework independently found repeated-run citation Jaccard medians of 0.29-0.50 across Gemini, SearchGPT, and Perplexity and showed apparent citation-share gaps dissolving under overlapping confidence intervals. This is exactly why the planned design's rerun-variance-as-null-baseline is the correct and defensible move: a language effect should be claimed only where EN-vs-Hinglish overlap falls detectably below same-language rerun overlap, and with 5 repetitions per cell the study should report bootstrap or Wilson intervals and confine headline claims to coarse outcomes (brand present/absent, top-3 membership, answer-language classification) rather than fine-grained share differences.

On the specific question of prompt language, the literature offers one direct precedent and one clear gap. Chen, Wang, Chen and Koudas (2025) is the only prior GEO work that translated queries across languages and measured citation shifts: they found language moves cited-domain sets more than paraphrasing does, with strongly engine-specific behavior (GPT switches site ecosystems by language, Claude stays English-heavy, Perplexity tilts toward target-language sites), and a systematic earned-media bias in all AI engines (up to 92% third-party sources vs Google's balanced mix). Żatuchin (2026) ran the nearest design twin — 250 category queries x 3 engines x 5 reps — and found the three engines agree on the top recommended brand in only 41.6% of queries, but tested English only. Nothing in this literature tests romanized code-mixed Hinglish, purchase-intent prompts, or India geolocation; the study therefore fills a real gap while being able to borrow every metric, the cross-language Jaccard analysis, the multi-run null baseline, and the rank-shift statistics from named prior work rather than inventing methodology.

### GEO: Generative Engine Optimization
- **Authors/venue:** Pranjal Aggarwal, Vishvak Murahari, Tanmay Rajpurohit, Ashwin Kalyan, Karthik Narasimhan, Ameet Deshpande — ACM SIGKDD 2024 (Barcelona); arXiv:2311.09735 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09735
- **Key findings:** Coined GEO and defined the field's canonical visibility metrics. (1) Word-count impression: Imp_wc(c_i,r) = sum of lengths of sentences citing source c_i divided by total response length. (2) Position-adjusted word count: Imp_pwc(c_i,r) = sum over citing sentences of |s|*e^(-pos(s)/|S|), normalized by total response word count, so earlier sentences carry exponentially more weight; word credit is split equally when a sentence cites multiple sources. (3) Subjective impression: GPT-3.5 rates seven G-Eval-style dimensions (relevance, influence on response, uniqueness, subjective position, subjective content volume, click likelihood, diversity), normalized to the same mean/variance as position-adjusted word count. Content tweaks (adding quotations +41%, statistics +33%, fluency +29% on position-adjusted word count) boost source visibility; keyword stuffing does not. Effects replicated on live Perplexity.ai (200-query subset: quotations +22%/+32%).
- **Methodology notes:** Simulated engine: top-5 Google results fed to gpt-3.5-turbo at temperature 0.7; 5 responses sampled per query 'to reduce statistical deviations'; experiments repeated over 5 random seeds with averages reported and standard deviations in appendix; no formal significance tests. Metric formulas extracted from the full HTML version (arxiv.org/html/2311.09735v3).
- **Relevance to this study:** The metric toolbox to adapt: replace 'source' with 'brand' and Imp_pwc becomes a position-weighted brand share-of-answer usable per prompt-language-engine cell. The 5-samples-per-query practice is the closest precedent for the study's 5 repetitions per cell, and the paper's own caveat that efficacy 'varies across domains' motivates splitting skincare vs fashion. Its LLM-judge subjective metric is optional garnish; the objective word-count metrics are cheap and reproducible from captured answers.

### GEO-bench (official dataset release for the GEO paper)
- **Authors/venue:** GEO-Optim (Aggarwal, Murahari et al.) — Hugging Face Datasets (2024), benchmark
- **URL:** https://huggingface.co/datasets/GEO-Optim/geo-bench
- **Key findings:** 10,000 queries (8K/1K/1K train/val/test) drawn from nine sources (MS MARCO, Natural Questions, ORCAS-I, AllSouls, LIMA, Davinci-Debate, Perplexity Discover, ELI5, GPT-4-generated), each paired with cleaned text of the top-5 Google results, tagged across ~25 domains and 7 query categorizations including intent.
- **Methodology notes:** Dataset card confirms per-query structure (query + 5 cleaned sources) and the tag taxonomy; supports both objective and subjective impression scoring.
- **Relevance to this study:** Its query taxonomy (intent, difficulty, domain tags) is a template for documenting the 10 purchase-intent prompts. Note the gap the study fills: GEO-bench is English-only and not commerce-focused; there is no code-mixed or India-geolocated slice, which is precisely the study's contribution.

### C-SEO Bench: Does Conversational SEO Work?
- **Authors/venue:** Haritz Puerto, Martin Gubri, Tommaso Green, Seong Joon Oh, Sangdoo Yun — NeurIPS 2025 Datasets & Benchmarks Track; arXiv:2506.11097 (2025), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.11097
- **Key findings:** First multi-domain, multi-actor benchmark of conversational SEO. Visibility is quantified as the change in a document's citation rank within the engine's answer after applying a C-SEO method. Two tasks (web QA: news/debates/general web; product recommendation: retail products, video games, books). Headline results: most published C-SEO methods are ineffective or hurt ranking; traditional SEO transfers better; and when multiple actors adopt the same optimization, gains collapse toward zero-sum (diminishing returns with adoption rate).
- **Methodology notes:** 16,360 items across six domains (count per the GEO-Bench 2026 paper's reuse of the data); code at github.com/parameterlab/c-seo-bench, data at huggingface.co/datasets/parameterlab/c-seo-bench with results organized {partition}/{method}/{model}. Introduces an evaluation protocol that varies the fraction of adopting actors.
- **Relevance to this study:** Validates product recommendation as a first-class GEO task and supplies a rank-in-answer metric: for each brand, record its rank order within the recommendation list, not just presence. Its multi-actor framing also suggests reporting brand-level results relative to competitors in the same answer, which is how the EN-vs-Hinglish deltas should be expressed (rank shifts, not absolute mentions).

### Ranking Manipulation for Conversational Search Engines
- **Authors/venue:** Samuel Pfrommer, Yatong Bai, Tanmay Gautam, Somayeh Sojoudi (UC Berkeley) — EMNLP 2024 main conference; arXiv:2406.03589 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2024.emnlp-main.534/
- **Key findings:** Formalizes conversational search ranking as an adversarial ordering problem over retrieved product websites and introduces a dataset of real consumer product sites. A tree-of-attacks prompt-injection technique reliably promotes low-ranked products, and the attacks transfer to production Perplexity.ai. Demonstrates that the ordering of products inside an LLM answer is a measurable, manipulable quantity with direct financial stakes.
- **Methodology notes:** Real-world consumer product website dataset; rank of the target product in the generated recommendation list is the outcome variable; code at github.com/spfrommer/cse-ranking-manipulation. arXiv record: https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.03589.
- **Relevance to this study:** Gives the study its dependent-variable framing for fashion/skincare prompts: treat each answer as a ranked brand list and measure rank movement across language arms. Also a reason to capture and archive the cited source pages at run time, since answer rankings are sensitive to what the retrieval layer surfaced that day.

### Don't Measure Once: Measuring Visibility in AI Search (GEO)
- **Authors/venue:** Julius Schulte, Malte Bleeker, Philipp Kaufmann (University of St. Gallen; Aurora Intelligence) — arXiv:2604.07585 (submitted April 2026) (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.07585
- **Key findings:** Single-shot visibility measurements are statistically unreliable. Across ChatGPT, Gemini, Google AI Mode, and Perplexity over 45 days: day-over-day cited-source Jaccard was only 0.34-0.42 (roughly 65% turnover), and even simultaneous identical reruns overlapped only 0.32-0.43 Jaccard, proving most instability is stochastic generation, not the web changing. Brand mentions are more stable than citations (Jaccard 0.45-0.59). Convergence analysis recommends 7-8 runs per prompt per day and 21-28 day rolling windows before trusting per-brand detection rates or trends.
- **Methodology notes:** 8 prompts per campaign across 4 verticals (telecom, real estate, sporting goods, consumer electronics); brand detection via campaign lexicons of 32-51 brands with substring matching; metrics: Jaccard, Rank-Biased Overlap (p=0.9), Gini coefficient for citation concentration; bootstrap subsampling (2,000 iterations), 95% CIs. Full numbers from arxiv.org/html/2604.07585v1.
- **Relevance to this study:** The methodological backbone for the study's null baseline. The right test is: EN-vs-Hinglish overlap (Jaccard/RBO on brands and on cited domains) compared against within-language rerun overlap from the 5 repetitions; a language effect exists only if cross-language overlap is significantly below rerun overlap. Also a sober warning: 5 reps per cell is below their 7-8 run recommendation, so per-brand detection rates should be reported with intervals, not as point facts.

### Quantifying Uncertainty in AI Visibility: A Statistical Framework for Generative Search Measurement
- **Authors/venue:** Ronald Sielinski (IQRush) — arXiv:2603.08924v2 (stat.AP) (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2603.08924v2
- **Key findings:** Treats citation share, citation count, and citation prevalence as random variables and builds response-level bootstrap 95% CIs. Empirically (Perplexity, SearchGPT, Gemini; three shopping-adjacent topics; ~200 queries/topic/platform over 9 days plus 10-minute-interval sampling): repeated-run domain Jaccard medians of 0.29-0.31 (Gemini), 0.33-0.40 (SearchGPT), ~0.50 (Perplexity); citation distributions follow a power law; a motivating example shows a 9.5% vs 6.0% citation-share gap dissolving under overlapping CIs. Sample-size guidance for a 0.05-wide citation-share CI: ~40-50 queries (Gemini), ~90-100 (Perplexity), 150+ (SearchGPT, which also shows non-stationarity within samples).
- **Methodology notes:** Bootstrap resampling of whole responses with replacement (preserves within-response dependency); SHA-256 checksums of cited pages confirm variance comes from engine behavior, not source churn; industry-affiliated single author, disclosed methodology.
- **Relevance to this study:** Prescribes the exact statistical treatment for a 5-rep design: bootstrap at the response level, report CIs on brand-mention share, and refuse to headline differences whose intervals overlap. Its sample-size tables imply the 10-prompt study can credibly support coarse claims (mention/no-mention, large rank shifts, answer-language classification) but not fine-grained citation-share differences; write the limitations section accordingly.

### Who Owns the AI Recommendation? A Multi-Industry Empirical Map of Brand Category Ownership Across Large Language Models
- **Authors/venue:** Dmitrij Żatuchin (Estonian Entrepreneurship University of Applied Sciences; Rankfor.AI) — arXiv:2606.23057 (June 2026) (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23057v1
- **Key findings:** Maps brand-recommendation behavior with 250 brand-free category queries x 3 models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, Perplexity sonar-pro) x 5 repetitions (3,750 calls). Defines reusable metrics: Category Ownership Index (brand mention frequency / total iterations, with dominance thresholds), Recommendation Share (mean COI per industry), Competitive Vacuum Index (1 - max COI), and Displacement Score (asymmetric substitution when a rival is absent). Findings: brand concentration is moderate (mean Gini 0.28, CI [0.16, 0.41]), only 8% of queries lack a dominant brand, and the three models agree on the top brand in just 41.6% of queries.
- **Methodology notes:** English-only queries; API access rather than web-UI capture; 5 repetitions per query-model cell is the same repetition count as the planned study; no geographic conditioning.
- **Relevance to this study:** The closest existing design to the planned study (category queries x 3 engines x 5 reps) and the source of directly liftable metrics: COI per brand per language arm, and cross-model agreement rate as a headline statistic. Its 41.6% cross-model agreement figure predicts engine will matter at least as much as language. Explicitly English-only with no geography, so an India-geolocated EN/Hinglish replication is a genuine, citable gap.

### Generative Engine Optimization: How to Dominate AI Search
- **Authors/venue:** Mahe Chen, Xiaoxuan Wang, Kaiwen Chen, Nick Koudas — arXiv:2509.08919 (September 2025) (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.08919
- **Key findings:** Roughly 1,000 consumer ranking prompts across 10 verticals run on Perplexity (sonar-pro, search on), Claude 3.5 Sonnet with web search, Gemini 2.5 Flash with grounding, and gpt-4o-search-preview, benchmarked against Google. The only prior GEO work with a cross-language arm: base queries translated into Chinese, Japanese, German, French, Spanish, then cited-domain sets compared via Jaccard overlap. Languages shift citations more than paraphrases do (7 paraphrase templates produced 0.3-0.7 base-variant overlap; language switches produced far lower overlap, with GPT effectively switching site ecosystems by language while Claude stayed English-heavy). AI engines show overwhelming earned-media bias: for example 92.1% earned sources in US consumer electronics vs Google's 51.7%, with social sources near zero.
- **Methodology notes:** Jaccard on unique cited domains per query, averaged to vertical level; source-type coding into Brand/Earned/Social; regional (US/Canada) splits; no repetition-count or CI reporting found in the extracted sections. Full design details from arxiv.org/html/2509.08919v1.
- **Relevance to this study:** Direct precedent and directional prediction for the EN-vs-Hinglish comparison: prompt language demonstrably changes WHICH domains get cited and whether citations tilt toward target-language sites, with the effect size differing per engine. Reuse their per-engine cross-language Jaccard analysis and their Brand/Earned/Social source coding for the citation arm of the study. Crucially, they tested clean translations into major languages; romanized code-mixed Hinglish is untested territory, and their engine-specific asymmetries (GPT most language-sensitive) are hypotheses the study can check.

### GEO-Bench: Benchmarking Ranking Manipulation in Generative Engine Optimization
- **Authors/venue:** Ojas Nimase, Zhe Chen, Gengpei Qi, Yue Zhao (USC); Xiyang Hu (ASU) — arXiv:2605.29107v2 (May 2026) (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2605.29107
- **Key findings:** Unifies evaluation of eight ranking-manipulation algorithms and ten white-hat strategies across five datasets (including C-SEO Bench's 16,360 items). Standardizes rank-movement metrics: Normalized Rank Gain (NRG), Success@alpha (target reaches top-alpha fraction of the ranking), Promote@alpha (promotion into top-alpha from outside), plus stealth metrics (Keyword Violation Rate, Perplexity Ratio). Finds black-box content rewriting matches gradient-based attacks on promotion with better fluency; no attack wins on promotion, stealth, and fluency simultaneously. Distinct from Aggarwal et al.'s GEO-bench despite the name.
- **Methodology notes:** Single target model (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct), which limits external validity to production engines; metrics are cleanly specified and portable.
- **Relevance to this study:** Supplies formalized rank-shift metrics the study can adopt without inventing anything: report NRG and Success@3 (brand appears in top-3 recommendations) per brand between EN and Hinglish arms. Also useful for terminology hygiene: cite it to disambiguate 'GEO-bench' (Aggarwal 2024 dataset) from 'GEO-Bench' (this 2026 manipulation benchmark) so reviewers do not conflate them.


## Lens: answer-stability

The academic literature is unambiguous that a single LLM run is a sample from a distribution, not a measurement. Even under nominally deterministic settings (temperature 0, fixed seed), outputs vary across identical calls: Atil et al. (arXiv:2408.04667) found accuracy swings up to 15% across 10 reruns of the same task on production APIs, Blackwell et al. (arXiv:2410.03492) showed determinism cannot be assumed and proposed cheap uncertainty quantification from repeated runs, and Pinhanez et al. (arXiv:2509.09705) found that even on multiple-choice questions only 50-80% of items get the same answer across 10 trials from smaller models. The self-consistency line of work (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) established the standard remedy: aggregate over several samples per prompt, with most of the benefit arriving in the first handful. Consumer web UIs (ChatGPT, Gemini) run at unknown nonzero temperature with retrieval in the loop, so the study's surfaces sit at the high-variance end of everything measured in this literature.

For the study's specific outcomes, the recommendation-focused work gives concrete null baselines. Unusual.ai's rerun-stability paper (arXiv:2605.27440) is the key precedent: identical commercial buying prompts rerun the same day (N=30) overlap at only Jaccard 0.50-0.61 on recommended-brand sets, and any prompt manipulation must be judged against that floor — their paraphrases landed 21-48 points below it, showing prompt surface form (which is what an EN/Hinglish swap changes) can move recommendations far more than rerun noise. Żatuchin's category-ownership audit (arXiv:2606.23057) provides direct precedent for exactly 5 repetitions per query-model cell across the same three engine families, aggregating brand mentions over reps; it also found engines agree on the top brand in only 41.6% of queries, so engine identity will likely dwarf language as a variance source. Citations are far noisier than brands: SE Ranking measured just 9.2% three-way exact-URL overlap across three same-day runs of Google AI Mode, Profound measured 40-60% cited-domain drift over one month across ~80k prompts per engine (Perplexity most stable at 40.5%), and Advanced Web Ranking found only 49% of brands held visibility across three weekly checks.

The defensible design posture that falls out of this: 5 reps per cell is at the low end of academic practice (10 is more common) but has a direct published precedent in brand-recommendation auditing and is adequate if the analysis aggregates correctly — compute within-language, within-engine rerun overlap (Jaccard on brand sets, appearance-rate per brand) as the explicit null baseline, run all reps in a tight time window so temporal drift cannot impersonate a language effect, bootstrap confidence intervals over reps and prompts rather than reporting point estimates, and only claim an EN-vs-Hinglish effect where cross-language divergence exceeds within-language rerun divergence. Expect brand-set overlap around 0.5-0.6 within a cell, near-total URL-level citation churn (forcing domain-level, union-over-reps citation analysis), and per-engine baselines reported separately, with skincare and fashion split since vertical strongly modulates volatility.

### Non-Determinism of "Deterministic" LLM Settings
- **Authors/venue:** Berk Atil, Sarp Aykent, Alexa Chittams, Lisheng Fu, Rebecca J. Passonneau, et al. (incl. Breck Baldwin) — arXiv preprint (cs.CL) (2024 (rev. 2025)), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667
- **Key findings:** Five LLMs run with fully deterministic settings (temperature 0, fixed seed) still produce different outputs across runs. Accuracy varied up to 15% across naturally occurring repeated runs, with best-to-worst output gaps up to 70%, and no model delivered repeatable accuracy on all tasks. Introduces TARr@N (total agreement rate on raw output) and TARa@N (agreement on parsed answers); raw-output agreement is frequently far below 100%. Attributes instability partly to batched inference on shared serving infrastructure, meaning it will persist in production APIs.
- **Methodology notes:** 5 LLMs x 8 tasks, 10 repetitions per condition, zero-shot and few-shot; agreement measured with TARr@N and TARa@N over the 10 runs.
- **Relevance to this study:** Establishes that rerun variance exists even under the most deterministic settings available, so variance in ChatGPT/Gemini web UIs (no temperature control, default sampling) is guaranteed to be nonzero and must be measured, not assumed away. Justifies the study's core design decision to treat rerun variance as the null baseline against which any EN-vs-Hinglish difference is tested. Its 10-rep protocol is a useful anchor: 5 reps is half that, defensible for effect detection but not for precise per-prompt stability estimation.

### Paraphrase Brittleness in Production Retrieval-Augmented Commercial Recommendation: Reproducibility Below the Rerun-Stability Baseline
- **Authors/venue:** Will Jack, Noah Lehman, Keller Maloney, Sarah Xu (Unusual.ai) — arXiv preprint (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2605.27440
- **Key findings:** Formalizes exactly the null-baseline logic this study needs: first measure how much recommendation sets churn on identical reruns, then ask whether a prompt manipulation exceeds that churn. Same-prompt, same-day, within-cell Jaccard overlap of recommended-brand sets was only 0.50-0.61, i.e. roughly half the brand set changes between identical runs. Cosmetic paraphrases dropped Jaccard to 0.288 (95% CI 0.215-0.361) and constraint-adding paraphrases to 0.135 (CI 0.098-0.175), 21-48 points below the rerun baseline. Reasoning-effort level had negligible effect on stability (bounded within -0.015 to +0.005).
- **Methodology notes:** Rerun baseline: 50 commercial prompts x 4 model cells (gpt-5.4-mini, claude-sonnet-4-6, each at low/high reasoning) x N=30 identical same-day reruns = 6,000 runs. Paraphrase arm: ~20 base prompts x ~5 variants across 5 axes x 3 cells x N=20 repeats (~6,000 runs). Brands extracted via dual-judge consensus (claude-haiku-4-5 + gpt-5-mini); Jaccard similarity on recommendation sets; bootstrap CIs.
- **Relevance to this study:** The single most directly transferable design. It supplies a concrete, citable expectation for the null: identical buying prompts rerun the same day overlap at Jaccard ~0.5-0.6 on brand sets. An EN/Hinglish pair is a strong cross-lingual paraphrase, so the study should expect language-pair Jaccard well below the rerun baseline if language matters, and it should compute within-language rerun Jaccard from its 5 reps as the comparison floor. Notably, one of its paraphrase axes was region/language bindings, making the Hinglish arm a natural extension. Also cautions that 5 reps yields noisier Jaccard estimates than their N=20-30, so aggregate across the 10 prompts rather than reporting per-prompt stability.

### Who Owns the AI Recommendation? A Multi-Industry Empirical Map of Brand Category Ownership Across Large Language Models
- **Authors/venue:** Dmitrij Żatuchin — arXiv preprint (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/html/2606.23057
- **Key findings:** 3,750 responses across 250 brand-free category queries, 5 industries, 3 models (GPT-5.2, Gemini 3 Flash, Perplexity sonar-pro), each query repeated 5 times under a 'dice roll' stability protocol. Brand concentration was moderate rather than winner-takes-all (mean Gini 0.28, 95% CI 0.16-0.41). Cross-model agreement on the top-recommended brand occurred in only 41.6% of queries (64.4% mean pairwise agreement), i.e. which brand 'wins' depends heavily on the engine. Proposes Category Ownership Index, Displacement Score, and Competitive Vacuum Index as repetition-aware metrics.
- **Methodology notes:** 5 repetitions per query-model cell, temperature 0.3, identical system prompts; 250 queries x 3 models x 5 reps = 3,750 calls; brand mention frequency aggregated over the 5 reps.
- **Relevance to this study:** Direct precedent for exactly 5 repetitions per cell in a brand-recommendation audit across the same trio of engine families (OpenAI, Gemini, Perplexity Sonar) — citable justification for the study's rep count. Its mention-frequency-over-5-reps aggregation (a brand 'owns' a query only if it recurs across reps) is the right unit of analysis for the EN/Hinglish comparison. The 41.6% cross-model top-brand agreement also predicts that engine will be a larger source of variance than language, so the study should model engine and language effects separately rather than pooling.

### Self-Consistency Improves Chain of Thought Reasoning in Language Models
- **Authors/venue:** Xuezhi Wang, Jason Wei, Dale Schuurmans, Quoc Le, Ed Chi, Sharan Narang, Aakanksha Chowdhery, Denny Zhou — ICLR 2023 (2022 (ICLR 2023)), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.11171
- **Key findings:** The canonical demonstration that sampled LLM outputs to one prompt disagree substantially with each other, and that aggregating over multiple samples (majority vote over sampled reasoning paths) is far more reliable than any single run: +17.9% on GSM8K, +11.0% on SVAMP, +12.2% on AQuA over greedy single-run decoding.
- **Methodology notes:** Samples multiple (typically 5-40) reasoning paths per question at nonzero temperature and marginalizes by taking the most frequent final answer; evaluated on arithmetic and commonsense benchmarks across PaLM/GPT-class models.
- **Relevance to this study:** The peer-reviewed academic anchor for two claims in the study's methods section: (1) a single run of a generative model is a sample, not 'the answer', so single-run brand comparisons are methodologically invalid; (2) aggregation across repetitions (e.g. a brand counts as recommended for a cell if it appears in a majority of the 5 reps) is the established remedy. Its finding that most of the aggregation gain arrives within the first handful of samples supports 5 reps as a reasonable cost-accuracy point.

### Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks
- **Authors/venue:** Lovish Madaan et al. (Meta / UCL) — arXiv preprint (2024), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.10229
- **Key findings:** Systematically measures variance components (seed variance across training initializations, monotonicity during training, bootstrapped 95% CIs) across standard LLM benchmarks and a large set of models. Shows variance differs widely by benchmark, that some benchmarks have low signal-to-noise, and that performance differences smaller than the measured variance band are not meaningful.
- **Methodology notes:** Empirical variance estimates over many open and from-scratch-pretrained models; reports per-benchmark variance magnitudes with bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals; proposes variance-reduction techniques for choice tasks.
- **Relevance to this study:** Supplies the statistical framing the study needs to be publicly defensible: report an uncertainty band from the 5 reps (bootstrap over reps and prompts) and only claim an EN-vs-Hinglish effect when the between-language gap exceeds that band. Useful citation for reviewers asking why the study reports CIs on share-of-voice rather than point estimates from single runs.

### Towards Reproducible LLM Evaluation: Quantifying Uncertainty in LLM Benchmark Scores
- **Authors/venue:** Robert E. Blackwell, Jon Barry, Anthony G. Cohn — arXiv preprint (2024 (rev. 2025)), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.03492
- **Key findings:** Shows LLMs remain stochastic even at temperature 0 with fixed seeds, that most published evaluations skip uncertainty quantification for cost reasons, and proposes a simple, cost-effective method for estimating the uncertainty of a score from a modest number of repeated runs, with practical reporting guidance.
- **Methodology notes:** Repeated experimental runs on cardinal-direction reasoning benchmarks across multiple models; examines how repetition count affects score stability and confidence-interval width; explicitly addresses the budget-vs-precision tradeoff in choosing repetition counts.
- **Relevance to this study:** The best citation for the '5 reps per cell' budget decision: it legitimizes small-N repetition schemes provided uncertainty is estimated and reported rather than pretending determinism. For a 10-prompt x 2-language x 3-engine x 5-rep grid (300 runs), the study can follow this paper's approach of reporting per-cell score plus CI, and acknowledging that 5 reps bounds but does not eliminate estimation noise.

### The Non-Determinism of Small LLMs: Evidence of Low Answer Consistency in Repetition Trials of Standard Multiple-Choice Benchmarks
- **Authors/venue:** Claudio Pinhanez, Paulo Cavalin, Cassia Sanctos, Marcelo Grave, Yago Primerano (IBM Research) — arXiv preprint (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.09705
- **Key findings:** Using 10 repetition trials of identical multiple-choice questions, only 50-80% of questions get answered consistently by small models (2B-8B) even at low temperatures; medium models (50-80B) are substantially more consistent. Consistency varies strongly by model and temperature, and accuracy computed on the consistent subset correlates with, but differs from, overall accuracy.
- **Methodology notes:** 10 repeated trials per question on MMLU-Redux and MedQA; multiple temperatures; finetuned vs base variants; consistency = fraction of questions with identical answers across all trials.
- **Relevance to this study:** Even on constrained multiple-choice outputs, 20-50% of items flip across reruns, so free-form buying recommendations (a much larger output space) should be expected to churn at least that much, consistent with the ~0.5 Jaccard rerun baselines seen in recommendation studies. Also warns that consistency itself is model-dependent, supporting the study's per-engine (rather than pooled) variance baselines, and that reporting the share of prompt-cells with unanimous top-brand agreement across the 5 reps is a meaningful headline metric.

### AI Search Volatility: Why AI search results keep changing
- **Authors/venue:** Profound (research team) — Profound blog (tryprofound.com) (2025), industry-study
- **URL:** https://www.tryprofound.com/blog/ai-search-volatility
- **Key findings:** Across ~80,000 prompts per platform re-asked one month apart (June-July 2025), the share of cited domains that were new (citation drift) was 59.3% for Google AI Overviews, 54.1% for ChatGPT, 53.4% for Copilot, and 40.5% for Perplexity. Over six months (Jan-Jul), drift grew roughly linearly to 70-90%. Headline: 40-60% of the domains cited for an identical question are different one month later; single-snapshot measurement charts noise.
- **Methodology notes:** ~80,000 prompts per platform, identical queries re-run at a one-month interval; drift = % of domains in later responses absent from earlier ones; per-engine breakdown; largest disclosed-methodology industry dataset found for citation churn.
- **Relevance to this study:** Sets expectations for the WHAT-sources-are-cited outcome: citations are the least stable layer, far noisier than brand mentions. Two consequences for the study: (1) run all 5 reps of every cell within a tight window (same day or two) so temporal drift does not masquerade as a language effect; (2) analyze citations at domain level, not URL level, and expect even the rerun baseline for citation overlap to be low. Perplexity's lower drift suggests engine-specific citation-stability baselines are needed.

### AI Mode Research: Sources, Volatility, & Differences between AIO and Organic Search
- **Authors/venue:** SE Ranking research team — SE Ranking blog (seranking.com) (2025), industry-study
- **URL:** https://seranking.com/blog/ai-mode-research/
- **Key findings:** 10,000 keywords run through Google AI Mode three times on the same day (June 20, 2025, US, logged-out): three-way exact-URL overlap across all runs was only 9.2% (Jaccard), domain-level 14.7%; pairwise overlap between any two runs was 18.5-19% for URLs and 26.6-27% for domains. AI Mode overlapped AI Overviews at only 10.7% (URLs) and top-10 organic at 14%.
- **Methodology notes:** 10,000-keyword set, 3 independent same-day parses, non-logged-in US sessions, Jaccard coefficient on cited sources; overlap also computed vs AI Overviews and organic top 10.
- **Relevance to this study:** The starkest same-day, identical-query churn number in the industry literature: ~91% of exact cited URLs differ across three same-day runs of a Gemini-family answer engine. Directly calibrates the study's Gemini citation analysis: with 5 reps, expect near-total URL-level churn within a single language, so the EN-vs-Hinglish citation comparison must use domain-level union sets across reps, and any 'Hinglish cites different sources' claim must beat this brutal within-language baseline.

### AI Brand Mention Volatility Study: 481 Sites Across ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews
- **Authors/venue:** Advanced Web Ranking (author-attributed single-analyst study) — Advanced Web Ranking blog (2025), industry-study
- **URL:** https://www.advancedwebranking.com/blog/ai-brand-mention-volatility-study
- **Key findings:** Tracking 481 websites across finance, education, health, and SaaS (20 keywords per niche) weekly for three weeks on ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Google AI Overviews: only 49% of brands kept consistent visibility across all three weeks. Churn varied by vertical (finance 34.7% dropout vs health 10.7%) and by authority (78% of DA 70+ brands stayed stable; 64% of DA<50 sites dropped at least once). Engines showed different source profiles (ChatGPT skewed to publishers, 62% of mentions).

- **Relevance to this study:** Week-scale brand-presence churn of ~50% frames the WHICH-brands outcome: even in English-only, half of mentioned brands are unstable over weeks, and volatility differs sharply by vertical, which matters since the study spans two verticals (skincare vs fashion) and should report them separately. Its confound (weekly cadence mixes drift and noise) is precisely what the study's same-window 5-rep design fixes, a contrast worth stating in the write-up.


## Lens: multilingual-disparity

The academic record gives strong, convergent grounds to expect that query language changes all three of the study's outcomes, but through two distinct mechanisms that the study should keep separate. First, the parametric-knowledge mechanism: multilingual models do not store the same facts across languages, and consistency does not improve with scale (Qi et al., EMNLP 2023), yet the interpretability line of work (Wendler et al., ACL 2024; Schut et al., 2025) shows models route semantically loaded decisions through an English-shaped concept space regardless of prompt language. Together these predict partial convergence: brand SETS for English and Hinglish twins should overlap more than naive "different language, different knowledge" reasoning implies, with residual divergence concentrated in locally-known Indian brands that are weakly represented in English training text. Because Hinglish is Latin-script Hindi, Park and Lee's finding that generators prefer query-language or Latin-script evidence suggests Hinglish prompts may behave more like English than Devanagari Hindi would, which is a citable rationale for the study's Hinglish-first design and a caveat on generalizing results to Hindi-script users.

Second, the retrieval-and-citation mechanism, which applies directly to Perplexity Sonar and to ChatGPT/Gemini in web-search mode: query language causally shapes which documents are retrieved and cited even when relevance is held constant. Faux Polyglot (NAACL 2025) shows same-language preference with fallback to English when query-language documents are scarce; Linguistic Nepotism (2025) shows models trade citation relevance for language preference, worse for low-resource languages; Chirkova et al. (2024) show citation behavior is far more variable in lower-resource languages; and Peec AI's 10M-prompt industry analysis shows ChatGPT executes 43% of its background fan-out searches in English for non-English prompts, with at least 78% of non-English sessions touching English sources. The concrete prediction for the study: Hinglish prompts will largely surface English-language citations anyway (convergence at the source level), Indian-language publishers will be near-invisible in both arms, and any citation-language differences will be smaller than the fan-out mechanism alone would suggest. Answer language is itself an unstable outcome worth measuring: the Language Confusion Benchmark (EMNLP 2024) shows even strong models fail to reliably reply in the user's language, and the code-switching literature (Zhang et al., EMNLP 2023; COMI-LINGUA, 2025) shows Hinglish is a genuinely harder input class, though frontier closed models now handle it credibly, so comprehension failure cannot be assumed as the explanation for divergence.

On measurement, the literature is blunt about what a 10-prompt, 5-repetition design can and cannot claim. SparkToro/Gumshoe's 2,961-run study found identical brand lists recur in under 1% of reruns of the SAME prompt, while per-brand appearance rates were stable (55-77% for category leaders), and the LLM-nondeterminism literature confirms outputs vary even at temperature 0. The design's rerun-variance-as-null-baseline is therefore the right instinct, but with n=5 per cell the study should report brand presence/visibility rates pooled across the 5 prompts per vertical (GEO's position-adjusted visibility metrics are the standard vocabulary), test EN-vs-Hinglish differences against within-language rerun variance, and avoid any per-run or rank-based claims. Liu et al.'s verifiability audit (only 74.5% of citations in generative search engines actually support their sentence) argues for reporting cited domains as displayed sources rather than verified provenance. No prior study was found that measures EN-vs-Hinglish effects on brand recommendations in consumer AI search; the closest work covers European languages (Peec) or monolingual low-resource queries (academic mRAG), so the proposed study occupies a real gap, and I found no existing Hinglish AI-search study to either build on or contradict.

### Cross-Lingual Consistency of Factual Knowledge in Multilingual Language Models
- **Authors/venue:** Jirui Qi, Raquel Fernández, Arianna Bisazza — EMNLP 2023 (Outstanding Paper, Multilinguality track) (2023), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.10378
- **Key findings:** Introduces the RankC metric to measure whether a model ranks candidate facts the same way across languages, independent of accuracy. Multilingual models store substantially different factual knowledge per language; scaling model size improves per-language accuracy but does NOT improve cross-lingual consistency. Knowledge edits inserted in English transfer only to languages with high RankC overlap with English.
- **Methodology notes:** Probing over the BMLAMA balanced multilingual fact benchmark across multiple multilingual PLMs; consistency measured pairwise between languages with RankC; model-editing follow-up experiment. Code and data public on GitHub.
- **Relevance to this study:** Direct theoretical grounding for the core hypothesis: if parametric knowledge itself is language-inconsistent, the brand set surfaced for a Hinglish prompt can legitimately differ from its English twin even before retrieval enters. Justifies treating EN-vs-Hinglish brand-set overlap as the primary outcome, and predicts Hinglish (low-resource, romanized) should show lower consistency with English than high-resource pairs.

### Do Llamas Work in English? On the Latent Language of Multilingual Transformers
- **Authors/venue:** Chris Wendler, Veniamin Veselovsky, Giovanni Monea, Robert West — ACL 2024 (long paper) (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.820/
- **Key findings:** Using a logit lens on Llama-2, shows middle-layer representations of non-English prompts decode preferentially to the ENGLISH version of the semantically correct next token before shifting to the input language in final layers. The abstract 'concept space' sits closer to English than to other languages, implying English-centric bias in what the model retrieves conceptually.
- **Methodology notes:** Carefully constructed non-English prompts with unique correct single-token continuations; intermediate-embedding trajectory analysis across layers of the Llama-2 family. Code public (epfl-dlab/llm-latent-language). arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.10588
- **Relevance to this study:** The mechanistic 'why' behind any observed convergence: if models pivot through English internally, English and Hinglish prompts may retrieve overlapping brand concepts, so the study should expect partial (not total) divergence. Gives the study a citable mechanism when explaining whichever direction the results go.

### Do Multilingual LLMs Think In English?
- **Authors/venue:** Lisa Schut, Yarin Gal, Sebastian Farquhar — arXiv preprint (Oxford/DeepMind line of work) (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.15603
- **Key findings:** Extends the latent-English finding to decision-making: LLMs make key lexical/semantic decisions in a representation space closest to English regardless of input/output language, emitting English-like representations for semantically loaded words (exactly the category brand names fall into) before translating out. Steering vectors computed in English work better than ones computed in the prompt language.
- **Methodology notes:** Logit-lens analysis over French, German, Dutch, Mandarin sentences plus activation-steering experiments; open-weight models. Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed at time of review.
- **Relevance to this study:** Strengthens the prior that semantically loaded choices (which brand to recommend) are computed in an English-shaped space, predicting brand overlap between EN and Hinglish variants may be higher than surface-language differences suggest, while answer phrasing/language still diverges. Useful for framing the null hypothesis.

### Faux Polyglot: A Study on Information Disparity in Multilingual Large Language Models
- **Authors/venue:** Nikhil Sharma, Kenton Murray, Ziang Xiao — NAACL 2025 (long paper) (2025), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.05502
- **Key findings:** In RAG settings, LLMs systematically prefer documents in the same language as the query, for both retrieval and generation, and for both factual and opinion queries. When query-language documents are scarce, they fall back to high-resource (English) sources, creating 'information cocoons' and reinforcing dominant-language views.
- **Methodology notes:** Controlled RAG simulation with parallel multilingual document sets and factual + opinion queries; ACL Anthology version: https://aclanthology.org/2025.naacl-long.411/. Abstract does not disclose full language/query counts; treat effect sizes from full paper before citing numbers.
- **Relevance to this study:** The single most on-point academic prediction for the citation-source outcome: Hinglish prompts (romanized, low-resource) should trigger the fallback path to English sources, so cited domains for EN and Hinglish prompts may converge on English-language sites while Hindi-language publishers stay invisible. Motivates coding cited sources by language and country.

### Retrieval-augmented generation in multilingual settings
- **Authors/venue:** Nadezhda Chirkova, David Rau, Hervé Déjean, Thibault Formal, Stéphane Clinchant, Vassilina Nikoulina — KnowLLM Workshop @ ACL 2024 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.01463
- **Key findings:** Builds mRAG across 13 languages (queries and datastores). Off-the-shelf multilingual retrievers and generators are not enough: task-specific prompt engineering is required just to get the model to ANSWER in the user's language, and citation incorporation is much more variable for lower-resource languages.
- **Methodology notes:** 13-language mRAG pipeline study; component-wise evaluation of retriever, reranker, generator; ACL Anthology PDF: https://aclanthology.org/2024.knowllm-1.15.pdf
- **Relevance to this study:** Direct support for measuring answer language as a first-class outcome variable, not an afterthought: even well-built pipelines drift back to English. Also predicts higher run-to-run variance in citations for the Hinglish arm, which the 5-repetition design can quantify against the EN arm.

### Investigating Language Preference of Multilingual RAG Systems
- **Authors/venue:** Jeonghyun Park, Hwanhee Lee — Findings of ACL 2025 (2025), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.11175
- **Key findings:** Decomposes language preference by pipeline stage: retrievers prefer high-resource languages and the query language; generators prefer the query language or Latin-script documents, producing inconsistent outputs when multilingual evidence conflicts. Proposes DKM-RAG (translated-passage fusion) as mitigation.
- **Methodology notes:** Systematic stage-wise experiments on mRAG; ACL Anthology: https://aclanthology.org/2025.findings-acl.295/. Abstract omits exact model/language counts; full paper needed for effect sizes.

### Linguistic Nepotism: Trading-off Quality for Language Preference in Multilingual RAG
- **Authors/venue:** Dayeon Ki, Marine Carpuat, Paul McNamee, Daniel Khashabi, Eugene Yang, Dawn Lawrie, Kevin Duh — arXiv preprint (abstract page notes ICML 2026 spotlight) (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13930

## Lens: hinglish-nlp

A decade of code-mixed NLP research established that Hinglish, which in real usage is overwhelmingly romanized rather than Devanagari, is a distinct register that standard multilingual models handle poorly. The foundational benchmarks (GLUECoS at ACL 2020, LinCE at LREC 2020) built evaluation infrastructure over romanized Hindi-English social media text and showed that multilingual pretrained models degrade on code-switched input, especially on semantic tasks like NLI and QA, unless explicitly adapted with code-switched data. The generation-side work (HinGE, HinglishEval at INLG 2022) added a second caution directly relevant to prompt construction: for any given English-Hindi sentence pair there are many acceptable Hinglish renderings, human annotators disagree substantially about their quality, and automatic systems struggle to predict those ratings. This means the "semantically equivalent EN/Hinglish pair" in the study design is a nontrivial artifact that should be validated (e.g., by bilingual review and by controlling code-mixing density with a metric like CMI), not assumed.

The LLM-era evidence is remarkably consistent about the direction of the effect: code-mixed and romanized input is harder for current models than English. Zhang et al. (EMNLP 2023) showed multilingual LLMs under zero/few-shot prompting underperform much smaller fine-tuned models on code-switched tasks; CodeMixBench (EMNLP 2025) found consistent underperformance across 18 languages; Indi-RomCoM (2026) found near-universal accuracy drops on romanized code-mixed instructions that grow monotonically with code-mixing density (e.g., roughly 7-8 points at 75% mixing even for frontier and Indic-specialized models, with small models losing up to about 17 points); and Script Gap (2025) measured up to 24-point drops on romanized vs native-script messages in a real-world Indian health triage deployment. Crucially, neither model scale nor Indic-focused pretraining closes the gap. At the same time, romanization is not uniformly harmful: RomanSetu (ACL 2024) showed romanized text can match or beat native script as an LLM interface once models are adapted, because romanized Hindi shares tokens with English and aligns more closely with English embeddings. So for consumer assistants the net effect of a Hinglish prompt on answer quality, brand selection, and retrieval behavior is genuinely uncertain, which is exactly what the study measures. The Cohere language confusion work (EMNLP 2024) separately established that even the strongest models fail to consistently reply in the language of the prompt, and that confusion worsens with complex prompts and higher sampling temperature, which both motivates the "what language does the answer come back in" outcome variable and justifies using rerun variance as the null baseline.

The gap this study fills is real: nothing in this literature measures commercially consequential outputs (which brands get recommended, which sources get cited) under code-mixed prompts. Existing work measures classification accuracy, translation quality, or instruction-following scores; the closest analogues are Indi-RomCoM's instruction tasks and Script Gap's triage decisions, both of which show that romanized code-mixed input changes model behavior, not just fluency. Three design implications follow. First, write the Hinglish prompts as romanized code-mix at a realistic, roughly constant mixing density, since degradation scales with density and romanized text is the empirically hardest and most ecologically valid condition (no Devanagari, matching real Indian user behavior and the project's own Hinglish-first decision). Second, 5 repetitions per cell is a defensible minimum given documented output instability, but report per-cell dispersion (brand-set overlap across reruns) before attributing any EN-vs-Hinglish difference to language, since temperature-driven variance is a known confound. Third, expect asymmetric effects by task type: the literature predicts Hinglish prompts may shift retrieval and citation behavior (Perplexity/Gemini web search grounding) more than raw comprehension, since comprehension of light code-mix is largely intact in frontier models while generation and retrieval behaviors are where language effects surface.

### GLUECoS: An Evaluation Benchmark for Code-Switched NLP
- **Authors/venue:** Simran Khanuja, Sandipan Dandapat, Anirudh Srinivasan, Sunayana Sitaram, Monojit Choudhury — ACL 2020 (Microsoft Research) (2020), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.329/
- **Key findings:** First broad benchmark for code-switched NLP: 11 datasets spanning 6 tasks (LID, POS, NER, sentiment, QA, and a new code-switched NLI task) in English-Hindi and English-Spanish, with the Hindi-English data largely romanized social-media text. Multilingual models (mBERT-class) beat cross-lingual ones, but performance drops on semantic tasks; fine-tuning on artificially generated code-switched data gives the best results in most tasks, showing generic multilingual pretraining does not transfer cleanly to code-mix. Companion site and scripts at https://microsoft.github.io/GLUECoS/ and arXiv:2004.12376.
- **Methodology notes:** Standard per-task metrics (F1/accuracy) over 11 public datasets; released preprocessing and evaluation scripts for reproducibility; no LLM-era models (pre-GPT-3 evaluation).
- **Relevance to this study:** Establishes that romanized Hindi-English is the canonical form of Hinglish in NLP evaluation and that models systematically find it harder than monolingual input, especially on meaning-level tasks. Grounds the study's choice of romanized (no Devanagari) Hinglish prompts and predicts language-conditional behavior differences are plausible, not exotic.

### LinCE: A Centralized Benchmark for Linguistic Code-switching Evaluation
- **Authors/venue:** Gustavo Aguilar, Sudipta Kar, Thamar Solorio — LREC 2020 (2020), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2020.lrec-1.223/
- **Key findings:** Combines 10 corpora across 4 code-switched language pairs including Hindi-English (romanized) and 4 tasks (LID, NER, POS, sentiment), with a live leaderboard. ### HinGE: A Dataset for Generation and Evaluation of Code-Mixed Hinglish Text
- **Authors/venue:** Vivek Srivastava, Mayank Singh — Eval4NLP workshop at EMNLP 2021 (2021), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2021.eval4nlp-1.20/
- **Key findings:** 1,976 English-Hindi sentence pairs with 6,694 human-generated Hinglish renderings (at least two per pair) plus 2,766 synthetic Hinglish sentences from two rule-based algorithms (WAC, PAC), each quality-rated by two human annotators. Shows there are many valid Hinglish realizations of the same meaning and that synthetic code-mix is often rated poorly by humans. Also at arXiv:2107.03760.
- **Methodology notes:** Two annotators per synthetic sentence rating quality; multiple human references per source pair; dataset released for generation and evaluation research.
- **Relevance to this study:** Directly informs construction of the 10 semantically-equivalent EN/Hinglish prompt pairs: multiple Hinglish renderings exist per English prompt, so the study should document how each pair was written and ideally have a bilingual speaker verify naturalness, since an unnatural Hinglish prompt is a confound for any brand-shift finding.

### HinglishEval Generation Challenge on Quality Estimation of Synthetic Code-Mixed Text: Overview and Results
- **Authors/venue:** Vivek Srivastava, Mayank Singh — INLG 2022 Generation Challenges (2022), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2022.inlg-genchal.3/
- **Key findings:** Shared task on predicting human quality ratings (1-10 scale) and annotator disagreement (0-9 scale) for synthetic Hinglish built on HinGE; 23 teams from 18 institutions registered. Systems found quality prediction hard, and annotator disagreement itself was a prediction target, confirming that what counts as good Hinglish is contested even among humans. Task site: https://sites.google.com/view/hinglisheval.
- **Methodology notes:** Two subtasks (quality rating prediction, disagreement prediction); human-annotated gold labels from two annotators; leaderboard-style comparison of 23 teams.
- **Relevance to this study:** Warns that Hinglish quality is subjective: the study should not treat its Hinglish prompts as uniquely correct translations. Publishing the exact prompt pairs (as the study plans) is the right transparency move, and citing HinglishEval preempts reviewer objections about prompt equivalence.

### Multilingual Large Language Models Are Not (Yet) Code-Switchers
- **Authors/venue:** Ruochen Zhang, Samuel Cahyawijaya, Jan Christian Blaise Cruz, Genta Indra Winata, Alham Fikri Aji — EMNLP 2023 (2023), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.774/
- **Key findings:** First comprehensive LLM-era audit of code-switching: benchmarks a range of multilingual LLMs under zero- and few-shot prompting across sentiment analysis, machine translation, summarization, and word-level LID on code-switched data (including Hindi-English). LLMs show promise on some tasks but significantly underperform fine-tuned models of much smaller scale; the authors conclude multilingualism does not imply code-switching proficiency. Also at arXiv:2305.14235.
- **Methodology notes:** Zero/few-shot prompting protocol compared against fine-tuned baselines across four task families; established code-switching corpora (LinCE-family datasets) as test beds.
- **Relevance to this study:** The key citation for the study's core hypothesis: even strong multilingual LLMs behave differently on code-switched input than their monolingual performance suggests, so expecting Hinglish buying prompts to elicit different behavior from ChatGPT/Gemini/Perplexity than English prompts is well-grounded.

### MEGA: Multilingual Evaluation of Generative AI
- **Authors/venue:** Kabir Ahuja, Harshita Diddee, Rishav Hada, Millicent Ochieng, Krithika Ramesh, et al. (Microsoft) — EMNLP 2023 (2023), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2023.emnlp-main.258/
- **Key findings:** First large benchmarking of generative LLMs (GPT-4, ChatGPT vs prior SOTA) on 16 NLP datasets across 70 typologically diverse languages, including Indic languages. Generative models lag SOTA on low-resource and non-Latin-script languages, with tokenization identified as a major factor. Also at arXiv:2303.12528; code at https://github.com/microsoft/Multilingual-Evaluation-of-Generative-AI-MEGA.
- **Methodology notes:** 16 datasets, 70 languages; standardized prompting strategies (monolingual, translate-test, cross-lingual); comparison against fine-tuned SOTA per dataset.
- **Relevance to this study:** Provides the broader baseline that GPT-family models are measurably weaker in Indic languages than English, motivating why an India-geolocated, Hindi-adjacent prompt study of consumer assistants is worth running at all; also a methodological reference for disclosing prompting templates.

### RomanSetu: Efficiently unlocking multilingual capabilities of Large Language Models via Romanization
- **Authors/venue:** Jaavid Aktar Husain, Raj Dabre, Aswanth Kumar, Jay Gala, Thanmay Jayakumar, Ratish Puduppully, Anoop Kunchukuttan — ACL 2024 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.833/
- **Key findings:** Using romanized text as the interface to an English-centric LLM (Llama 2 continually pretrained and instruction-tuned on romanized data) reduces token fertility 2x-4x versus native script and matches or outperforms native-script representation across NLU, NLG, and MT tasks; romanized-text embeddings align more closely with English translations than native-script embeddings do. Also at arXiv:2401.14280.
- **Methodology notes:** Continual pretraining plus instruction tuning on romanized Indic text; evaluation across NLU/NLG/MT suites; token-fertility and embedding-alignment analyses. Companion interpretability work: RomanLens, arXiv:2502.07424.
- **Relevance to this study:** The counterweight finding: romanized Hindi is not inherently the degraded condition; for adapted models it can be the more efficient interface because it shares tokens with English. This means the study's EN-vs-Hinglish deltas could go either direction per engine, and per-engine differences (e.g., Gemini vs Perplexity Sonar) are themselves an interesting result rather than noise.

### Understanding and Mitigating Language Confusion in LLMs
- **Authors/venue:** Kelly Marchisio, Wei-Yin Ko, Alexandre Berard, Theo Dehaze, Sebastian Ruder (Cohere) — EMNLP 2024 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.20052
- **Key findings:** Introduces the Language Confusion Benchmark (LCB) over 15 typologically diverse languages (Hindi included, with a noted caveat that hi/ru were not run for the monolingual Okapi subset) measuring whether models reply in the user's desired language, at line level and word level. Llama Instruct and Mistral models show high confusion; even the strongest models fail to consistently respond in the correct language; confusion worsens with complex prompts and higher sampling temperature; few-shot prompting, multilingual SFT, and preference tuning partially mitigate. Code: https://github.com/for-ai/language-confusion.
- **Methodology notes:** Line-level and word-level pass-rate metrics over monolingual and cross-lingual generation settings; existing plus newly created English and multilingual prompt sets; multiple model families compared.
- **Relevance to this study:** The direct methodological precedent for the study's third outcome variable (what language the answer comes back in). Predicts Hinglish prompts will produce mixed or inconsistent answer languages, that this varies by model family, and that temperature-driven instability is real, which justifies the 5-repetition rerun-variance null baseline.

### COMI-LINGUA: Expert Annotated Large-Scale Dataset for Multitask NLP in Hindi-English Code-Mixing
- **Authors/venue:** Rajvee Sheth, Himanshu Beniwal, Mayank Singh — arXiv (cs.CL) (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.21670
- **Key findings:** Largest expert-annotated Hinglish resource to date: 125K+ instances and 376K+ annotations by three bilingual annotators (Fleiss kappa >= 0.81) covering matrix LID, token LID, POS, NER, and MT in both Devanagari and Roman scripts. Closed-source LLMs significantly outperform traditional tools and open-source models zero-shot (e.g., up to 95.25 F1 on NER, 98.77 F1 on matrix LID); one-shot prompting consistently boosts performance.
- **Methodology notes:** Three annotators per instance; Fleiss kappa reported per task; zero-shot, one-shot, and fine-tuning evaluation regimes; dataset on Hugging Face.
- **Relevance to this study:** Shows current frontier LLMs comprehend Hinglish quite well at the token/analysis level, sharpening the study's framing: any EN-vs-Hinglish brand or citation shift is unlikely to be raw comprehension failure and more plausibly reflects retrieval, grounding, or response-policy differences, which is exactly what an AEO/GEO study should claim.

### CodeMixBench: Evaluating Code-Mixing Capabilities of LLMs Across 18 Languages
- **Authors/venue:** Yilun Yang, Yekun Chai — EMNLP 2025 (2025), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.18791
- **Key findings:** Benchmark of 8 tasks (3 LLM-specific, 5 traditional NLP) across 18 languages from 7 language families, built with a synthetic generation pipeline combining word substitution and GPT-4 prompting. Finds consistent LLM underperformance on code-mixed datasets, especially when mixing crosses language families; training data size, model scale, and few-shot prompting help but do not close the gap. Code and data on GitHub.
- **Methodology notes:** Synthetic code-mix generation validated against human data; cross-family vs within-family mixing comparisons; multiple model scales tested.
- **Relevance to this study:** The most current peer-reviewed evidence that code-mixed degradation persists in the ChatGPT-class model generation the study will test, and that it is not an artifact of one language pair; supports generalizing from the study's Hindi-English findings with appropriate caveats.

### Indi-RomCoM: Code-Mixed Benchmark for Evaluating LLMs on Romanized Indic-English Instructions
- **Authors/venue:** Avisha Das, Mihir Parmar, Mohana Ramnath, Pulkit Verma — arXiv (cs.CL), submitted June 2026 (2026), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.30790
- **Key findings:** Stress-tests LLMs on romanized code-mixed (RCM) instructions: 7 instruction-following tasks, 4 Indic languages, 3 controlled code-mixing intensity levels, across proprietary, open-weight, and Indic-focused models in zero- and few-shot settings. Nearly all models degrade on RCM input, monotonically with code-mixing density; per the paper's results, even frontier models lose roughly 7-8 accuracy points at the 75% mixing level (e.g., Claude Opus 4.6 from 68.7 to 61.2; Indic-pretrained Sarvam-30B from 64.2 to 56.1) and small models lose up to about 17.5 points; detection-style tasks degrade more than reasoning tasks.
- **Methodology notes:** Controlled mixing-density manipulation (three levels) is the key design feature; zero-shot and few-shot configurations; reports per-model performance gaps relative to English baseline. Preprint, not yet peer-reviewed; exact run counts not stated in the abstract.
- **Relevance to this study:** The closest existing design to the study: instruction-style prompts, romanized Hindi-English, controlled density, frontier models, measured English-vs-code-mix gaps. Two direct takeaways: hold Hinglish prompt mixing density roughly constant across the 10 pairs, and expect model-dependent gap sizes, which makes the three-engine comparison a genuine contribution.

### Script Gap: Evaluating LLM Triage on Indian Languages in Native vs Romanized Scripts in a Real World Setting
- **Authors/venue:** Manurag Khullar, Utkarsh Desai, Poorva Malviya, Aman Dalmia, Zheyuan Ryan Shi — arXiv (cs.CL), submitted Dec 2025, revised Mar 2026 (2025), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.10780
- **Key findings:** On real user-generated maternal and newborn health queries in five Indian languages plus Nepali, leading LLMs perform substantially worse on romanized messages than native-script equivalents, with gaps up to 24 points across languages and models; the authors project nearly 2 million excess triage errors at their partner organization and propose uncertainty-based selective routing as mitigation.
- **Methodology notes:** Real-world deployment data rather than synthetic benchmarks; native vs romanized script as the controlled contrast; per-language and per-model gap reporting. Preprint; dataset size and run counts not in the abstract.
- **Relevance to this study:** The strongest real-world-consequences evidence that romanized Indic input changes LLM decisions, not just fluency, in a live Indian deployment. It is the best analogue for arguing that romanized Hinglish buying prompts could plausibly shift which brands get recommended, and it supports geolocating the study to India where romanized input dominates actual usage.


## Lens: india-ai-commerce

India is now the primary growth market for exactly the three assistants this study tests. Sam Altman put ChatGPT at 100 million weekly active users in India in February 2026 (OpenAI's second-largest market; largest student base), Bain's How India Shops Online 2026 independently cites 160M+ monthly actives by December 2025, Perplexity bought nationwide distribution by giving free 12-month Pro to Bharti Airtel's 360M subscribers (July 2025), and Google has pushed Gemini hard in India with free offers and a Google-Kantar adoption study. But adoption is early and demographically skewed: Google-Kantar's 8,000-person face-to-face survey (18 cities, ages 18-44, April 2025) found only 31% had ever tried a GenAI tool. The realistic Indian AI shopper in 2026 is urban, young, bilingual, and price-sensitive, which is precisely the persona whose language choice (English vs Hinglish) the study manipulates. Capgemini's 12,000-consumer, 12-country survey (India included) found 58% of consumers had replaced traditional search with GenAI for product recommendations, up from 25% in 2023, so purchase-intent prompting of assistants is an established behavior, not a hypothetical.

The English/Hinglish contrast is grounded in strong evidence that romanized code-mix is the default register of this adopter base, not an edge case. A peer-reviewed Nature Humanities & Social Sciences Communications study of 262,578 tweets from 16,710 Delhi/Mumbai users found Hinglish rose from 42% to ~60% of posts between 2015 and 2020, with 56.3% of users preferring Hinglish by 2022. A 2026 JMIR paper shows real urban Indian users spontaneously write code-mixed Hinglish queries to LLM-based systems and that even frontier models degrade on them (GPT-5 hierarchical F1 of 0.784 on intent classification, with open-weight and Indic models worse) — direct support for the study's expectation that prompt language can shift model behavior, and for using rerun variance as the null baseline since code-mix handling is noisier. Older but foundational industry research (KPMG-Google 2017: 9 of 10 new internet users are Indian-language users, with e-commerce queries in Indian languages growing faster than English in clothing and BPC; IAMAI-Kantar 2024: 886M internet users, 55% rural, ~57% of urban users preferring Indian-language content) establishes that vernacular-register commerce is a structural feature of Indian internet growth, extended today through voice and romanized text rather than Devanagari.

On the commerce side, beauty/personal care and fashion are the right categories and the brand ground truth is well documented: Redseer and Nykaa-commissioned work sizes India's BPC market at ~$21B heading to $34B by 2028 and $40B by 2030, with online BPC scaling ~2.4x in three years and quick commerce jumping from ~2% to ~16% of online BPC; Bain puts e-retail at $65-66B GMV in 2025 with fashion the largest discretionary category and Gen Z contributing 50% of incremental orders. Two developments bear directly on interpretation of results: Bain 2026 notes conversational commerce in India is nascent but gaining traction specifically in the research/comparison phase (where this study's prompts sit), and Nykaa signed a multi-year partnership with OpenAI (June 16, 2026) putting its beauty and fashion catalogue inside ChatGPT via the Agentic Commerce Protocol — meaning ChatGPT web-UI answers to Indian skincare/fashion prompts may be structurally biased toward Nykaa inventory, which the study must log (record whether connected-app/shopping surfaces appear, and date-stamp all runs). One honest gap: no disclosed-methodology data on AI referral traffic to Indian retailers specifically was found; Adobe's rigorous AI-referral series (traffic to retail sites up 1,200% YoY by Feb 2025, later converting better than non-AI traffic) is US-only, and Indian coverage is limited to partnership announcements. That absence is itself a finding: a geolocated India study measuring which brands and sources assistants surface would be filling a genuinely empty evidence slot. Throughout, sources split cleanly: peer-reviewed (Nature HSSC, JMIR) and the NBER working paper are disclosed-methodology research; Bain, Redseer, Google-Kantar, Capgemini, IAMAI-Kantar, KPMG-Google, and Adobe are industry studies with stated methods but commercial sponsors (Bain's is produced with Flipkart, Redseer's with Nykaa — treat their category framings accordingly); the Airtel and Nykaa-OpenAI items are marketing/press content useful only for factual deal terms and dates.

## Lens: measurement-stats

The statistics literature and the newer LLM-evaluation literature converge on one message for a 10-prompt x 3-engine x 2-language x 5-rep study: the design is workable, but only if inference is done at the right unit with small-sample methods. On proportions (brand-mention rates, citation-domain shares, answer-language rates), Brown, Cai and DasGupta (2001) is the canonical demonstration that Wald/CLT intervals fail at these sample sizes, and Bowyer et al. (ICML 2025) re-proves the point specifically for LLM evals under a few hundred datapoints, recommending exactly the Wilson-or-Bayesian intervals this study plans. Practically: per-cell n=5 proportions are almost pure noise (a 3/5 rate has a Wilson 95% CI of roughly 0.23-0.88), so all confirmatory claims must aggregate, per engine, across the 10 prompts and 5 reps, with prompts treated as clusters per Miller (2024), whose super-population framing, clustered standard errors, paired-differences recipe, and power formulas give the study its complete inferential skeleton.

For the headline set-overlap question, the right pattern combines two strands. Chung et al. (2019) establishes that a Jaccard coefficient is a statistic requiring a null reference and uncertainty (they supply bootstrap significance machinery and an R package); the LLM non-determinism strand (Ouyang et al. on ChatGPT reruns; Atil et al. showing up to 15% accuracy swings across 10 runs even at 'deterministic' settings; Song et al. on sampling spread) establishes that the correct null for this study is empirical rerun variance, i.e., rep-to-rep brand-set Jaccard within the same language and engine is the noise floor against which EN-vs-Hinglish Jaccard must be compared. The cleanest confirmatory test is then an exact paired permutation test across the 10 prompts (per-prompt statistic: cross-language overlap minus within-language rerun overlap, or paired difference in brand-mention rate), which with 2^10 = 1024 sign-flips is exact and assumption-free (Ernst 2004), and follows the standard NLP protocol for paired system comparisons (Dror et al., ACL 2018). Mizrahi et al. (TACL 2024) and Madaan et al. (2024) supply the variance-decomposition caveats: prompt-template variance is typically large relative to condition effects, so the study should report per-prompt results transparently and generalize only to prompts like these, not to all Indian buying queries.

Two honest framings follow. First, power: with 10 prompts as the effective inferential unit, only large language effects (on the order of 25-40 percentage-point shifts in brand-mention rates, or overlap gaps well outside the rerun-noise band) will reach significance; the study should therefore be framed primarily as ESTIMATION with intervals (Wilson CIs on proportions, bootstrap CIs on Jaccard, per-engine effect sizes) rather than as a null-hypothesis verdict, with the permutation test as a conservative headline check. Second, reporting: the REFORMS checklist (Kapoor et al., Science Advances 2024) plus Miller's reporting recommendations define what a credible public methodology section must disclose — exact prompt pairs, capture dates/window, geolocation mechanism, model/UI versions, brand-alias extraction rules fixed before analysis, every raw run (all 300), the rerun-variance baseline alongside every condition effect, and released code. Following that checklist is what will let this study be cited as evidence rather than dismissed as AEO marketing.

### Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations
- **Authors/venue:** Evan Miller (Anthropic) — arXiv preprint (widely cited; accompanied by Anthropic research blog post) (2024), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00640
- **Key findings:** Treats LLM evals as experiments drawn from an unseen super-population and imports standard experimental-analysis machinery: compute standard errors of the mean via CLT, use CLUSTERED standard errors when questions are drawn in related groups, reduce variance by resampling answers per question (K repetitions), and do inference on question-level PAIRED differences when comparing two conditions. Includes explicit power-analysis formulas for deciding how many questions/resamples are needed to detect a given effect size.
- **Methodology notes:** Analytic paper with worked formulas rather than a new benchmark; the resampling recommendation (multiple completions per item, variance decomposed into between-item and within-item components) and the paired-differences-with-clustered-SE recipe map one-to-one onto a prompts-as-clusters design. Recommends reporting standard errors and CIs, never bare point scores.
- **Relevance to this study:** This is the closest thing to a canonical citation for the study's whole inferential frame. The 10 prompts are the clusters, the 5 reps are the within-item resamples, and EN-vs-Hinglish is a paired comparison at the prompt level, so inference should run on prompt-level paired differences with clustered/aggregated variance, not on the 300 raw runs as if independent. Its power formulas also let the methodology section state honestly what effect sizes 10 prompts x 5 reps can and cannot detect.

### Position: Don't Use the CLT in LLM Evals With Fewer Than a Few Hundred Datapoints
- **Authors/venue:** Sam Bowyer, Laurence Aitchison, Desi R. Ivanova — ICML 2025 (spotlight position paper); PMLR v267 (2025), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01747
- **Key findings:** Shows empirically that CLT/Wald-style error bars dramatically underestimate uncertainty in LLM evals with fewer than a few hundred datapoints, and recommends easy-to-implement alternatives: frequentist intervals with correct small-sample coverage (Wilson-type) and Bayesian (Beta-Binomial) intervals, including treatment of paired model comparisons and clustered questions. Code released at github.com/sambowyer/no_clt_paper.
- **Methodology notes:** Coverage simulations across small-N regimes comparing Wald/CLT intervals against Wilson and Bayesian alternatives; explicit sections on paired comparisons and question clustering at small N. PMLR version: https://proceedings.mlr.press/v267/bowyer25a.html
- **Relevance to this study:** Directly governs this study's regime: per-cell n=5 and per-engine-per-language n=50 runs (25 per category) are exactly the 'fewer than a few hundred datapoints' setting. It is the modern, LLM-specific authority for why the study must use Wilson (or Bayesian) intervals rather than naive plus-or-minus-1.96-SE error bars, and it can be cited verbatim to justify the choice against a reviewer who asks why not t-tests.

### Interval Estimation for a Binomial Proportion
- **Authors/venue:** Lawrence D. Brown, T. Tony Cai, Anirban DasGupta — Statistical Science 16(2):101-133 (2001), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-16/issue-2/Interval-Estimation-for-a-Binomial-Proportion/10.1214/ss/1009213286.full
- **Key findings:** The canonical demonstration that the textbook Wald interval has chaotic, badly deficient coverage even at moderate n and p away from 0/1, and the standard recommendation of the Wilson score interval (or Jeffreys interval) for small n and Agresti-Coull for larger n.
- **Methodology notes:** Exhaustive coverage-probability analysis across n and p; DOI 10.1214/ss/1009213286. This is the reference statisticians expect to see next to any Wilson-interval claim.
- **Relevance to this study:** The primary citation for every proportion reported in the study: brand-mention rate (how often brand X appears across reps), citation-domain share, and answer-language rate per condition. With n as small as 5-50, Wald intervals would be indefensible; citing Brown/Cai/DasGupta plus Bowyer et al. makes the Wilson choice bulletproof. Also useful for the honest framing that a 3/5 per-cell rate carries a Wilson 95% CI of roughly 0.23-0.88, i.e. per-cell claims are off the table.

### Jaccard/Tanimoto Similarity Test and Estimation Methods for Biological Presence-Absence Data
- **Authors/venue:** Neo Christopher Chung, Blazej Miasojedow, Michal Startek, Anna Gambin — BMC Bioinformatics 20 (Suppl 15):644; also arXiv:1903.11372 (2019), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://bmcbioinformatics.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12859-019-3118-5
- **Key findings:** Provides statistical estimation and significance testing for Jaccard/Tanimoto coefficients on presence-absence (set) data: expected Jaccard under independence, centered coefficients, exact and asymptotic p-values, and a recommended BOOTSTRAP procedure for significance when exact computation is impractical. Ships as the open-source R package 'jaccard'.
- **Methodology notes:** Simulation studies show the bootstrap and measure-concentration approximations produce accurate p-values and FDR at a fraction of exact-solution cost. The key conceptual import: a raw Jaccard value is uninterpretable without a null expectation, because even independent sets share elements by chance.
- **Relevance to this study:** The study's headline metric, overlap between the brand set recommended under EN and under Hinglish, is a Jaccard statistic on small sets. This paper supplies (a) the citable case that raw Jaccard needs a null reference, and (b) bootstrap machinery for CIs/p-values. In our design the natural null is empirical rather than independence-based: within-condition rerun Jaccard (rep-to-rep overlap among the 5 same-language reruns) is the noise floor, and cross-language Jaccard is compared against that distribution, but this citation grounds the general treat-Jaccard-as-a-statistic-with-uncertainty stance.

### Permutation Methods: A Basis for Exact Inference
- **Authors/venue:** Michael D. Ernst — Statistical Science 19(4):676-685 (2004), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://projecteuclid.org/journals/statistical-science/volume-19/issue-4/Permutation-Methods-A-Basis-for-Exact-Inference/10.1214/088342304000000396.full
- **Key findings:** Accessible authoritative treatment of permutation/randomization tests: when they are exact and distribution-free, how to construct them for paired and two-sample designs, and why they are the method of choice when sample sizes are too small for asymptotic tests.
- **Methodology notes:** DOI 10.1214/088342304000000396. For a paired design with 10 units, the full sign-flip permutation distribution has 2^10 = 1024 elements, small enough to enumerate exactly, so the reported p-value is exact rather than approximate.
- **Relevance to this study:** The clean citation for the study's main hypothesis test: for each engine, compute a per-prompt statistic (e.g., cross-language brand-set Jaccard minus mean within-language rerun Jaccard, or difference in brand-mention rate) and run an exact paired sign-flip permutation test across the 10 prompts. No normality assumption, exact at N=10, and trivially explainable in a public methods section.

### The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in Natural Language Processing
- **Authors/venue:** Rotem Dror, Gili Baumer, Segev Shlomov, Roi Reichart — ACL 2018 (Proceedings of the 56th Annual Meeting of the ACL, Long Papers, pp. 1383-1392) (2018), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/P18-1128/
- **Key findings:** The standard NLP-community protocol for significance-test selection: use nonparametric tests (sign/McNemar, Wilcoxon, paired bootstrap, permutation/randomization) when metric distributions are unknown or samples are small; matches test type to evaluation-measure type; documents that a large share of ACL/TACL papers misuse or omit significance testing.
- **Methodology notes:** Survey plus decision-protocol paper; its decision tree explicitly routes small-sample, non-normal, paired NLP comparisons to exact nonparametric tests. This is the reference NLP reviewers expect for test-choice justification.
- **Relevance to this study:** Bridges the pure-statistics citations (Ernst, Brown et al.) to NLP practice: it lets the methodology section say the paired-permutation and bootstrap choices follow the established ACL protocol for paired system comparisons, which matters for a publicly citable study likely to be read by both marketing and NLP audiences.

### An Empirical Study of the Non-determinism of ChatGPT in Code Generation
- **Authors/venue:** Shuyin Ouyang, Jie M. Zhang, Mark Harman, Meng Wang — ACM Transactions on Software Engineering and Methodology; arXiv:2308.02828 (2023 (arXiv); 2025 (ACM TOSEM 34(2))), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.02828
- **Key findings:** Across 829 problems, identical prompts to ChatGPT yield highly unstable outputs across reruns; they quantify rerun instability with semantic, syntactic, and structural similarity measures across repeated requests, and show temperature=0 reduces but does NOT eliminate non-determinism.
- **Methodology notes:** Methodological template: fixed prompt, repeated requests, then pairwise similarity among the repetitions as the measured quantity. This 'similarity among reruns of the same condition' construct is exactly the study's null baseline, just with brand sets instead of code.
- **Relevance to this study:** Primary precedent for the study's core design decision to use rerun variance as the null: if rep-to-rep brand-set overlap within the SAME language is itself low, a low EN-vs-Hinglish overlap means nothing. Also citable for why 1 run per cell (common in AEO industry studies) is methodologically invalid, and why the study cannot assume web-UI ChatGPT is deterministic.

### Non-Determinism of "Deterministic" LLM Settings
- **Authors/venue:** Berk Atil, Sarp Aykent, Alexa Chittams, Lisheng Fu, Rebecca J. Passonneau, et al. — arXiv:2408.04667 (2024 (v5 2025; presented at Eval4NLP 2025)), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.04667
- **Key findings:** Five LLMs configured for maximum determinism, eight tasks, 10 runs each: accuracy still varied by up to 15% across runs, with best-to-worst-run gaps up to 70% on some tasks; no model delivered repeatable accuracy across all tasks, let alone identical output strings. Attributes residual non-determinism partly to batched/co-mingled inference serving, i.e. it is structural and not going away.
- **Methodology notes:** 10 repetitions per condition as the variance-measurement budget; reports spread (best/worst/across-run variance) rather than single scores. Useful as the closest published anchor for 'how many reps': they used 10; 5 is a defensible minimum if the analysis aggregates across 10 prompts, but the limitations section should acknowledge it.
- **Relevance to this study:** Strongest citation for the claim that even 'deterministic' settings do not remove run-to-run variance, so consumer web UIs (no temperature control at all) certainly will not. Directly supports the 5-repetitions-per-cell design and supports framing every per-cell number as a sample from a distribution. Also useful to preempt the objection 'why not just set temperature to 0 via API' for the Perplexity Sonar arm.

### The Good, The Bad, and The Greedy: Evaluation of LLMs Should Not Ignore Non-Determinism
- **Authors/venue:** Yifan Song, Guoyin Wang, Sujian Li, Bill Yuchen Lin — arXiv:2407.10457 (evaluated at NAACL-era benchmarks; code at github.com/Yifan-Song793/GoodBadGreedy) (2024), preprint
- **URL:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.10457
- **Key findings:** Single-output evaluation misrepresents model behavior: across benchmarks there are consistent, sizeable gaps between greedy decoding and sampled generations, and substantial spread across sampled completions of the same prompt; alignment (RLHF/DPO) reduces but does not remove sampling variance.
- **Methodology notes:** Runs many sampled completions per item and reports the distribution (and best-of-N) rather than one draw. Reinforces that the object of measurement for a sampled system is a per-prompt DISTRIBUTION over outputs.
- **Relevance to this study:** Since ChatGPT and Gemini web UIs sample at an unknown non-zero temperature, each of the study's cells is a 5-draw sample from a distribution over brand slates. This paper is the citation for reporting per-cell results as distributions (e.g., brand appears in k-of-5 reps) instead of treating any single capture as 'the answer', and for the claim that industry one-shot AEO screenshots are not evidence.

### State of What Art? A Call for Multi-Prompt LLM Evaluation
- **Authors/venue:** Moran Mizrahi, Guy Kaplan, Dan Malkin, Rotem Dror, Dafna Shahaf, Gabriel Stanovsky — Transactions of the ACL (TACL) 12:933-949; DOI 10.1162/tacl_a_00681 (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://aclanthology.org/2024.tacl-1.52/
- **Key findings:** Analysis of 6.5M instances across 20 LLMs and 39 tasks shows single-prompt-template evaluation is brittle: paraphrases of the same instruction change both absolute scores and MODEL RANKINGS. Proposes evaluating over a set of prompt paraphrases and reporting distribution-aware metrics.
- **Methodology notes:** Large-scale paraphrase collection; quantifies between-template variance as often comparable to between-model differences. Implication for small studies: prompt identity is a major variance component and must be treated as a random factor, not averaged away silently.
- **Relevance to this study:** Justifies the 10-prompt (5 skincare + 5 fashion) design over any single 'best prompt', and warns the limitations section: with 10 prompts, generalization is to these prompt types, not to 'all Indian buying queries'. Also supports analyzing prompts as the unit of inference (matching Miller's clustered-SE guidance) because between-prompt variance will likely dominate between-rep variance.

### REFORMS: Consensus-based Recommendations for Machine-learning-based Science
- **Authors/venue:** Sayash Kapoor, Emily Cantrell, Kenny Peng, et al. (19-researcher consensus, Princeton-led) — Science Advances 10(18):eadk3452 (preprint arXiv:2308.07832, 2023) (2024), peer-reviewed
- **URL:** https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/sciadv.adk3452
- **Key findings:** A 32-item, 8-module consensus checklist for reporting ML-based science, covering study goals, data provenance and collection dates, preprocessing, metric definitions fixed before analysis, uncertainty quantification, compute/version documentation, and public release of data and code to prevent the validity/reproducibility failures common in ML-adjacent studies.
- **Methodology notes:** Consensus/Delphi-style standards paper rather than an experiment; designed exactly for studies where ML systems are the measurement instrument. The checklist is short enough to adopt wholesale as an appendix.
- **Relevance to this study:** The credibility backbone for a publicly citable industry study: following REFORMS means disclosing exact prompt texts (both languages), capture dates and times, geolocation method (IP/VPN/account region), model/UI version identifiers, the full 300-run raw dataset, brand-extraction and alias-matching rules fixed BEFORE analysis, and all code. ### Quantifying Variance in Evaluation Benchmarks
- **Authors/venue:** Lovish Madaan, Aaditya K. Singh, Rylan Schaeffer, Andrew Poulton, Sanmi Koyejo, Pontus Stenetorp, Sharan Narang, Dieuwke Hupkes — arXiv:2406.10229 (Meta/Stanford/UCL; OpenReview: E2RyjrBMVZ) (2024), preprint

## Synthesis: methodology implications

# Literature Synthesis: Methodology Decisions for the EN-vs-Hinglish AI Search Study

## 1. What Prior Work Establishes

Five bodies of work converge on this study, and together they settle most of the methodological questions before a single prompt is run.

**A single LLM run is a sample, not a measurement.** This is the most heavily replicated finding across the entire review. Atil et al. (arXiv:2408.04667) showed accuracy swings up to 15% across ten reruns even at temperature 0 with fixed seeds, attributing residual non-determinism to batched serving infrastructure, meaning it is structural and will not go away. Ouyang et al. (ACM TOSEM, arXiv:2308.02828) established the methodological template of measuring pairwise similarity among reruns of an identical prompt. Song et al. (arXiv:2407.10457) showed single-output evaluation misrepresents sampled systems generally. For the specific object this study measures, recommendation sets, the numbers are stark: Unusual.ai (arXiv:2605.27440) found identical commercial buying prompts rerun the same day overlap at only Jaccard 0.50-0.61 on brand sets; SparkToro/Gumshoe found the identical brand list recurs in under 1% of 2,961 reruns while per-brand appearance rates stay stable at 55-77% for category leaders; St. Gallen's "Don't Measure Once" (arXiv:2604.07585) found simultaneous identical reruns overlap only 0.32-0.43 Jaccard on citations and 0.45-0.59 on brands. Citations are the noisiest layer of all: SE Ranking measured 9.2% three-way exact-URL overlap across three same-day Google AI Mode runs, and Profound measured 40-60% cited-domain drift over one month across ~80k prompts per engine. The consensus hierarchy of stability is: brand appearance rate > brand set > cited domain set > cited URL set, and every analysis choice below follows from it.

**Prompt language demonstrably moves what gets cited and recommended, through two separable mechanisms.** The parametric mechanism: multilingual models store different facts per language and scaling does not fix it (Qi et al., EMNLP 2023), yet models route semantically loaded decisions, which includes brand choice, through an English-shaped latent space (Wendler et al., ACL 2024; Schut et al., arXiv:2502.15603), predicting partial rather than total EN/Hinglish divergence. The retrieval mechanism: RAG systems prefer query-language documents with fallback to English when they are scarce (Faux Polyglot, NAACL 2025), sacrifice citation relevance for language preference, worst for low-resource languages (Linguistic Nepotism, arXiv:2509.13930), and generators specifically prefer Latin-script evidence (Park & Lee, Findings of ACL 2025) — a finding with a distinctive implication for Hinglish, which is Hindi in Latin script and may therefore behave more like English than Devanagari Hindi would. The one direct cross-language GEO precedent (Chen et al., arXiv:2509.08919) found language switches move cited-domain sets more than paraphrases do, with strongly engine-specific asymmetries. Industry-side, Peec AI's 10M-prompt analysis found ChatGPT executes 43% of background fan-out searches in English for non-English prompts, predicting citation-level convergence on English sources regardless of prompt language.

**Hinglish is a real, majority register and a genuinely harder input.** Peer-reviewed corpus work (Nature HSSC 2024) shows romanized Hinglish is the majority written register of urban Hindi-belt users (~60% of tweets, 56.3% of users by 2022), measurable via the Code-Mixing Index. The code-switching NLP literature from GLUECoS/LinCE through CodeMixBench (EMNLP 2025) and Indi-RomCoM (arXiv:2606.30790) shows consistent LLM degradation on romanized code-mix that grows monotonically with mixing density (7-8 points for frontier models at 75% mixing), while COMI-LINGUA shows frontier closed models nonetheless comprehend Hinglish well at the analysis level — so observed brand shifts are more plausibly retrieval/policy effects than comprehension failure. HinGE/HinglishEval establish that multiple valid Hinglish renderings exist per English sentence and that humans disagree about their quality, making prompt-pair construction itself a validity concern.

**The statistical treatment for small-N designs is settled.** Miller (arXiv:2411.00640) supplies the frame: prompts are clusters, repetitions are within-item resamples, and EN-vs-Hinglish is a paired comparison at the prompt level. Bowyer et al. (ICML 2025) prohibits CLT/Wald intervals below a few hundred datapoints and prescribes Wilson or Bayesian intervals; Brown/Cai/DasGupta (Statistical Science 2001) is the canonical backing. Ernst (2004) supplies exact paired permutation tests, enumerable at N=10 (2^10 = 1024 sign-flips); Dror et al. (ACL 2018) confirms this routing is the standard NLP protocol. Chung et al. (BMC Bioinformatics 2019) establishes that a raw Jaccard value is uninterpretable without a null reference and supplies bootstrap machinery.

**The market context makes the design commercially meaningful and introduces one specific confound.** India is ChatGPT's second-largest market (100M WAU), Perplexity has telco-scale distribution via Airtel, and Bain 2026 places conversational commerce exactly at the research/comparison stage the prompts simulate. The Nykaa-OpenAI Agentic Commerce Protocol deal (June 2026) means ChatGPT answers to Indian skincare/fashion prompts may be structurally tilted toward Nykaa inventory — an integration effect, not an organic model preference, that must be logged per run.

## 2. Methodology Decisions Forced or Supported by This Literature

1. **Primary hypothesis test: cross-language overlap vs. within-language rerun overlap, per engine.** For each prompt-engine cell, compute within-language rerun Jaccard on brand sets (pairwise among the 5 EN reps, and among the 5 Hinglish reps) as the empirical null floor, and cross-language Jaccard (EN reps vs Hinglish reps) as the treatment statistic. A language effect is claimed only where cross-language overlap falls detectably below same-language rerun overlap (Unusual.ai 2605.27440; St. Gallen 2604.07585; Chung et al. 2019 for the treat-Jaccard-as-a-statistic stance). Expect the null floor itself to sit around 0.5-0.6 for brand sets.

2. **Inference: exact paired sign-flip permutation test over the 10 prompts, per engine.** The per-prompt statistic is (cross-language Jaccard minus mean within-language Jaccard) or the difference in brand-mention rate. With N=10 the full 1024-element permutation distribution is enumerable, giving exact p-values with no distributional assumptions (Ernst 2004; Dror et al. 2018; Miller 2411.00640 for the prompts-as-clusters, paired-differences frame). Never run inference on the 300 raw runs as if independent.

3. **All proportions get Wilson 95% intervals, never Wald.** Brand-mention rate (k-of-5, k-of-25), citation-domain share, answer-language rate (Bowyer et al. 2503.01747; Brown/Cai/DasGupta 2001). State plainly that a 3/5 per-cell rate carries a Wilson CI of roughly 0.23-0.88, which is why no per-cell claims are made.

4. **Unit of analysis: brand appearance rate aggregated over reps, pooled within vertical, never per-run lists or ranks.** SparkToro/Gumshoe showed exact lists recur in <1% of reruns while appearance rates are stable; Żatuchin (2606.23057) provides the direct precedent of frequency-over-5-reps aggregation (his Category Ownership Index is directly liftable per language arm). Rank-based claims are restricted to coarse Success@3 (brand in top-3), borrowing the formalization from USC GEO-Bench (2605.29107) and the rank-in-answer framing from C-SEO Bench and Pfrommer et al. (EMNLP 2024).

5. **Adopt the GEO metric vocabulary for share-of-answer.** Word-count impression and position-adjusted word count (Imp_pwc with exponential position decay) from Aggarwal et al. (KDD 2024), computed with brands substituted for sources. These are cheap, objective, reproducible from captured text; skip the LLM-judge subjective metric.

6. **Citations analyzed at domain level, on union sets across reps, with Brand/Earned/Social coding.** URL-level analysis is precluded by the 9.2% same-day URL overlap baseline (SE Ranking); domain-level union-over-reps is the only stable object. Code source types per Chen et al. (2509.08919) and additionally code cited-source language and country (Faux Polyglot's prediction that Hinglish prompts will fall back to English sources is a headline testable claim). Report citations as "displayed sources," not verified provenance, since only ~74.5% of generative-engine citations actually support their sentence (Liu et al., Findings of EMNLP 2023).

7. **5 repetitions per cell is defensible but must be framed honestly.** Direct precedent: Żatuchin used exactly 5 reps across the same three engine families; Aggarwal et al. sampled 5 responses per query; self-consistency work (Wang et al., ICLR 2023) shows most aggregation benefit arrives in the first handful of samples. But 10 reps is the more common academic budget (Atil, Pinhanez) and St. Gallen recommends 7-8 for stable per-brand detection rates, so the limitations section must say 5 reps supports pooled effect detection, not per-prompt stability estimation (Blackwell et al. 2410.03492 legitimizes small-N repetition schemes provided uncertainty is reported).

8. **All 300 runs executed in a tight time window (ideally 1-2 days), date-stamped.** Profound's 40-60% monthly citation drift and AWR's ~50% three-week brand churn mean temporal drift will impersonate a language effect if reps are spread out. Interleave EN and Hinglish runs rather than running one arm then the other.

9. **Answer language is a first-class dependent variable with its own classifier and baseline.** Marchisio et al.'s Language Confusion Benchmark (EMNLP 2024) supplies line-level and word-level language-fidelity metrics; Chirkova et al. (2024) shows even engineered pipelines drift to English. Report per-engine answer-language distributions with Wilson intervals.

10. **Hinglish prompts constructed at roughly constant, documented code-mixing density, validated by bilingual review, with CMI reported per pair.** Indi-RomCoM shows degradation scales with mixing density, so uncontrolled density is a confound; HinGE/HinglishEval show Hinglish quality is contested, so publish the exact prompt pairs and the construction procedure. Romanized only, no Devanagari, matching both real usage (Nature HSSC) and ecological validity (Script Gap, 2512.10780).

11. **Per-engine everything; never pool engines.** Żatuchin's 41.6% cross-model top-brand agreement and Chen et al.'s engine-specific language sensitivity (GPT switches site ecosystems by language, others less so) predict engine identity is a variance source at least as large as language. Report skincare and fashion separately too, since vertical strongly modulates volatility (AWR; GEO paper's own domain caveat).

12. **Log run-context artifacts per capture:** full answer text, displayed citation list, presence of shopping cards or connected-app surfaces (the Nykaa-OpenAI ACP integration makes this mandatory for ChatGPT), engine/tier configuration (note Perplexity Sonar-with-search vs the Pro tier most Indian users have via Airtel), timestamp, and geolocation method. Archive cited pages at run time where feasible (Pfrommer et al.; Sielinski's SHA-256 checksum practice).

13. **Bootstrap at the response level for any share-type CI.** Resample whole responses with replacement, preserving within-response dependency (Sielinski, 2603.08924), 2,000 iterations following St. Gallen's practice.

14. **Pre-commit the claim tiers.** Given Sielinski's sample-size tables (~40-150 queries needed for a 0.05-wide citation-share CI), this study can headline only coarse outcomes: brand present/absent rates, top-3 membership, answer-language classification, and overlap-vs-null tests. Fine-grained citation-share differences are explicitly out of scope.

## 3. Gaps This Study Fills

The central gap is clean: **no prior work anywhere in this literature runs a controlled English-vs-Hinglish (or any code-mixed) comparison of purchase-intent prompts against production AI answer engines.** The nearest neighbors each miss by one dimension. Chen et al. (2509.08919) is the only cross-language GEO study, but tested clean translations into major high-resource languages (Chinese, Japanese, German, French, Spanish), never romanized code-mix, and reported no repetition counts or CIs. Żatuchin (2606.23057) is the design twin (category queries × 3 engines × 5 reps) but is explicitly English-only with no geographic conditioning. Peec AI's fan-out study covers European languages only, with no Hindi or Hinglish in the language set. The code-mixed NLP literature (Indi-RomCoM, Script Gap, CodeMixBench) measures classification accuracy, instruction-following, and triage decisions — never commercially consequential outputs like brand recommendations or citations. GEO-bench is English-only and not commerce-focused. And no disclosed-methodology dataset on AI-referral or AI-recommendation behavior for Indian retail exists at all; Adobe's series is US-only, and the India evidence base consists of partnership announcements. Secondary gaps: no prior study geolocates GEO measurement to India; none tests the Latin-script hypothesis (Park & Lee) on a real code-mixed register; and none combines web-UI capture of ChatGPT/Gemini with API-level Perplexity in one language-manipulation design. The study can therefore borrow every metric and test from named prior work while occupying genuinely empty territory — the strongest possible position for a publicly citable result.

## 4. Threats to Validity and Mitigations

**Rerun noise masquerading as a language effect.** The dominant threat. With within-cell brand-set Jaccard expected at 0.5-0.6 and citation overlap far lower, naive EN-vs-Hinglish comparisons will "find" differences everywhere. Mitigation: decisions 1-3 (empirical null floor, exact permutation test, Wilson intervals), and refuse to report any difference whose interval overlaps the null band.

**Temporal drift confounded with language.** Citations churn 40-60% monthly and brand visibility churns ~50% over three weeks. Mitigation: all runs in one tight window, arms interleaved, everything timestamped (decision 8).

**Prompt-pair non-equivalence.** If a Hinglish prompt is unnatural or shifts intent, brand differences reflect bad translation, not language. Mitigation: bilingual validation, constant CMI, published prompt pairs (decision 10); optionally a manipulation check that the engine restated the intent correctly, motivated by Zhang et al. (EMNLP 2023).

**Platform integrations biasing one arm's brand slate.** The Nykaa-OpenAI ACP deal can inject Nykaa into ChatGPT answers in exactly the two test categories. Mitigation: log shopping/connected-app surfaces per run and flag integration-attributable mentions separately (decision 12).

**Web-UI capture confounds.** Unknown temperature (language confusion worsens with temperature, per Marchisio et al.), possible personalization, session memory, and A/B-tested serving. Mitigation: fresh logged-out or clean-profile sessions, documented capture protocol, and explicit acknowledgment that web-UI variance is part of the measured construct, not a nuisance to be engineered away, with Perplexity's API arm noted as the one configurable surface.

**Citation lists misread as evidence provenance.** Only ~74.5% of citations support their sentence (Liu et al.). Mitigation: report "displayed sources," never causal claims about why a brand was recommended.

**Small-N overreach.** 10 prompts generalize to these prompt types, not "Indian buying queries" (Mizrahi et al., TACL 2024, shows prompt identity rivals model identity as a variance source). Mitigation: prompts treated as a random factor, claims scoped to the tested prompt classes, per-vertical reporting, and Miller-style power framing of what 10×5 can detect.

**Naming and terminology hazards.** Cite Aggarwal's GEO-bench and USC's GEO-Bench (2605.29107) with explicit disambiguation to avoid reviewer conflation.

**Population generalization.** The Hinglish register evidence is urban Hindi-belt (Delhi/Mumbai); Google-Kantar shows only ~31% GenAI trial in the surveyed population. Mitigation: frame findings as applying to the current urban, young, bilingual AI-shopper cohort, with the IAMAI-Kantar vernacular-majority data as the equity/readiness framing rather than a claimed sample frame.
